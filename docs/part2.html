Fair comparisons

Clean data can still mislead when two effects are mixed. This part first separates the effect of a place from the effect of the clock. It then chooses statistics that describe a typical minute even when short spikes occur, and it tests whether a strong correlation really means what it seems to mean. The last station shows how the dew point tells moister air apart from air that is only colder. All values of this part are computed from the table of clean minutes that Part 1 produced.

In short. The cleaned data are used to compare the places, but a comparison can easily mislead. As the morning warms up, the place measured last looks the warmest, although the cause is the clock time and not the place. For this reason the second round visits the same places in reverse order, and the two measurements are averaged. A few puffs of cigarette smoke at a door can inflate an average. A shaded place looks more humid, yet its air holds no more water, it is only cooler. When two things change together, this does not show that one causes the other. The main idea: A comparison that is not set up fairly leads to a wrong conclusion.

The last stop looks warm

In Round 1 on Campus A, the visit median of the green area is 13.9 °C and that of the road is 13.3 °C. Is the green area really warmer than the road?
In Round 1 the green area was the last stop at 12:05, while the road was the second stop at 10:25. The morning warmed by more than three degrees between these two visits. It was the clock, not the place, that made the green area look warm.

Round 1 visits the points from P1 to P6, and Round 2 visits them from P6 to P1 at the same clock times one week later. A point that is visited early in one round is therefore visited late in the other. The table below shows this for every point: The mean of the two start times is 11:02:30 for all six points. When the two rounds are averaged, every point thus receives the same mean clock time, and the warming of the morning affects all points equally.

xˉp​=2xp,1​+xp,2​​,Δp​=xˉp​−xˉref​

xp,1, xp,2 visit medians of point p in Rounds 1 and 2 · x̄p two-round mean · Δp difference from the quiet reference point (°C). Suppose the morning warms evenly, for example by 1 °C per hour. Because the two visits of every point have the same mean clock time, the warming adds the same amount to the two-round mean of every point. The reference point receives the same amount as well, so the warming cancels in Δp.

Worked example, Campus A. The values below are visit medians, computed from the clean minutes of Part 1. Green area: (13.91 + 8.34) / 2 = 11.12 °C. Road: (13.30 + 12.39) / 2 = 12.85 °C. Reference: (13.10 + 11.37) / 2 = 12.23 °C. So Δgreen = −1.11 °C and Δroad = +0.62 °C. Once the hour is balanced, the green area turns out to be cooler than the reference point, and the road warmer.

The reversal removes a trend that rises in a straight line. A curved trend, such as sunshine that peaks around noon, can leave a small remainder, and the field notes help to judge its size. Hard sunlit surfaces store heat, while trees shade the ground and cool the air by evaporation [1]. Part of the heat at the sunny square, however, comes from the sun warming the sensor housing itself.

Takeaway. Points should be compared through the two-round mean and through the difference from the reference point. A ranking taken from one round alone mixes the effect of the place with the effect of the hour.

Related or caused?

The correlation coefficient r measures how closely two quantities move together, on a scale from −1 to +1. A value near +1 means that one rises when the other rises, and a value near −1 that one falls when the other rises. A value near 0 means that there is no straight-line relation. This station computes r for temperature and air pressure over the 468 clean minutes of Part 1.

Over all 468 minutes, warmer minutes come with higher air pressure: r = 0.81. Does warm air raise the pressure?
The coastal Campus C is both warmer and lower than the others, so its minutes combine high temperature with high pressure. This alone creates the positive correlation over all minutes. Inside each campus and round the relation turns negative, because the pressure falls slowly during the morning while the air warms. The campus is the hidden third factor.

When a hidden third factor drives two variables at the same time, the correlation over all data can differ strongly from the correlations inside the groups. It can even reverse its sign. This is known as Simpson's paradox. Before a correlation is read as a cause, the data should therefore be split by the obvious groups, such as campus and round.

Wetter or only colder?

Relative humidity depends on temperature: The same air shows a higher relative humidity in a cooler place, although it holds no more water. A shaded point can therefore look wetter only because it is colder. The dew point removes this effect. It is the temperature to which the air would have to cool to become saturated. It therefore changes only when water vapor is added to the air or removed from it.

γ=ln100RH​+b+TaT​,Td​=a−γbγ​a=17.625,b=243.04 ∘C​

T temperature (°C) · RH relative humidity (%) · Td dew point (°C) · a, b constants of the Magnus formula, a widely used approximation that gives the dew point from temperature and relative humidity [2].

Worked example. Let T = 13.3 °C and RH = 43 %. The first term is ln(0.43) = −0.844, and the second is 17.625 × 13.3 / (243.04 + 13.3) = 0.914, so γ = 0.070. The dew point is then Td = 243.04 × 0.070 / (17.625 − 0.070) ≈ 1.0 °C. The air would therefore have to cool by about 12 degrees before dew forms.
