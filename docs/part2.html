Fair comparisons

Clean data can still mislead. This part separates the effect of a place from the effect of the clock. It also chooses statistics that resist spikes and tests whether a strong correlation means what it seems to mean.

The last stop looks warm

In Round 1 on Campus A, the green area reads 13.9 °C and the road reads 13.3 °C. Is the green area really warmer than the road?
The green area was the last stop at 12:05, the road the second at 10:25. The morning warmed by more than three degrees in between, so the clock, not the place, made the green area look warm.
The green area was the last stop at 12:05, the road the second at 10:25. The morning warmed by more than three degrees in between, so the clock, not the place, made the green area look warm.

Round 1 visits P1 to P6 and Round 2 visits P6 to P1 at the same clock times. A point visited early in one round is visited late in the other. Averaging the two rounds gives every point the same average clock time.

xˉp​=2xp,1​+xp,2​​,Δp​=xˉp​−xˉref​

xp,1, xp,2 visit medians of point p in Rounds 1 and 2 · x̄p two-round mean · Δp difference from the quiet reference point (°C). If the morning warms by b °C per hour, slot k of Round 1 and slot 5 − k of Round 2 add the same drift to every point. The drift cancels in Δp.

Worked example, Campus A. Green: (13.91 + 8.34) / 2 = 11.12 °C. Road: (13.30 + 12.39) / 2 = 12.85 °C. Reference: (13.10 + 11.37) / 2 = 12.23 °C. So Δgreen = −1.11 °C and Δroad = +0.62 °C: The green area is cooler after all.

The reversal removes a straight-line trend. A curved trend, such as sunshine that peaks around noon, can leave a small remainder, and the field notes help to judge it. Hard sunlit surfaces store heat while trees shade and cool by evaporation [1]. Part of the heat at the sunny square is also the sun warming the sensor housing.

Takeaway. Compare points through the two-round mean and through the difference from the reference point. A ranking from one round mixes the place with the hour.

Typical values, not only averages

At the busy student area, people smoke near the door. Where will the mean of PM2.5 sit compared with the median?
Short, strong plumes pull the mean up while the median stays with the typical minute. Turn on the means (white dots) to see the gap.

Each box collects the clean minutes of one point type from the three campuses and both rounds. The values are differences from the median of their own campus and round, so a cold coastal morning and a cold highland morning can sit in one box.

IQR=Q3​−Q1​,whiskers: [Q1​−1.5IQR,Q3​+1.5IQR]

Q1, Q3 first and third quartiles: A quarter of the minutes lie below Q1 and a quarter above Q3 · the line in the box is the median · circles beyond the whiskers are unusual minutes, not errors.

In everyday life: One person with a very high salary moves the average income of a street, but not its median. A smoking group does the same to the PM2.5 mean of a minute.

Related or caused?

Over all 468 minutes, warmer minutes come with higher air pressure: r = 0.81. Does warm air raise the pressure?
The coastal campus is both warmer and lower, so it has higher pressure. Inside each campus and round the relation turns negative, because the pressure falls slowly while the morning warms.

Wetter or only colder?

Relative humidity depends on temperature: The same air shows a higher relative humidity where it is cooler. The dew point removes this effect. It is the temperature at which the air would become saturated, so it changes only when water is added or removed.

γ=ln100RH​+b+TaT​,Td​=a−γbγ​,a=17.625,b=243.04 ∘C

T temperature (°C) · RH relative humidity (%) · Td dew point (°C) · a, b constants of the Magnus form [2].

Worked example. T = 13.3 °C and RH = 43 %. ln(0.43) = −0.844 and 17.625 × 13.3 / (243.04 + 13.3) = 0.914, so γ = 0.070. Td = 243.04 × 0.070 / (17.625 − 0.070) ≈ 1.0 °C.
