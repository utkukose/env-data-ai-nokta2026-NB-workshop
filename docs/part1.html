What the scene shows

One portable station visits the six points. It records for 15 minutes at each point and walks for 10 minutes to the next one. The column beside a point grows with the running median of the chosen channel. The dust cloud thickens with PM2.5, and the shadows follow the sun of that morning.

Takeaway. One station measures one place at a time. Every comparison between points is therefore also a comparison between clock times, and this shapes everything in Part 2.

A robust test for spikes

The usual z-score uses the mean and the standard deviation, but a large spike pulls both towards itself and hides. The modified z-score uses the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD), which a single spike cannot move [1].

Mi​=MAD0.6745(xi​−x~)​MAD=medianj​∣xj​−x~∣​

xi one reading (µg/m³) · x̃ median of the readings · MAD median of the distances to x̃ · the factor 0.6745 makes M comparable with an ordinary z-score. A reading with |M| above about 3 deserves a look.

Worked example. Seven PM2.5 readings: 14.2, 15.0, 13.8, 14.6, 97.3, 15.1, 14.4. The median is 14.6 and the distances to it are 0.4, 0.4, 0.8, 0.0, 82.7, 0.5, 0.2, so MAD = 0.4. For 97.3: M = 0.6745 × 82.7 / 0.4 = 139.45, a clear flag. The ordinary z-score gives mean 26.34, standard deviation 28.97 and z = (82.7 + 14.6 − 26.34) / 28.97 = 2.45, below 3: The spike hides itself.

The detector in the notebook adds two physical rules. A real plume lifts PM1, PM2.5 and PM10 together, while a glitch lifts one channel alone. CO₂ readings of 0 or 2000 ppm are out of range, and a CO₂ value that does not move for two minutes is a frozen sensor.

Three stations, one story

Before the field rounds, the three NOKTA Stations ran side by side for two hours. Any steady difference between them is a property of the devices, not of the campuses.

Station S2 reads about half a degree warmer than the others. Which station is right?
Without a calibrated reference instrument, no station can be declared right. The stations are aligned to their common mean. This removes the differences between devices, not an error they all share.

5

Humid air fools the particle counter

On the humid morning of Round 2 on Campus C, the raw PM2.5 of the green area is higher than at the road. Why?
The green area was the first stop, at 87 % relative humidity. Particles take up water, grow, and a light-scattering sensor without a dryer counts them as more mass [3, 4].
It is the air, not the place. The green area was the first stop, at 87 % relative humidity. Particles take up water and grow, and a light-scattering sensor without a dryer counts them as more mass [3, 4].
Relative humidity

C(aw​)=1+−1+1/aw​κρw​/ρp​​,aw​=100RH​PMcorrected​=CPMraw​​​

C growth factor of particle mass (no unit) · κ hygroscopicity of the particles, 0.3 as an average continental value · ρw/ρp density of water over the density of dry particles, 1/1.65 · RH relative humidity (%) [4].

Worked example. Green area, RH = 87.3 %, so aw = 0.873. Then −1 + 1/0.873 = 0.145 and 0.3/1.65 = 0.182. C = 1 + 0.182/0.145 = 2.255. The raw median 15.07 µg/m³ becomes 15.07/2.255 = 6.7 µg/m³.
rawcorrected

Campus C, Round 2, PM2.5 visit medians in µg/m³. After the correction the green area is the cleanest point again. The value of κ belongs to the local air, so a real study estimates it against a reference instrument.

Takeaway. A low-cost sensor reports what it sees, not always what is there [5]. Quality control, device alignment and physical corrections come before any AI method, and each step is written down.
