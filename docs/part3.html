AI finds patterns

Unsupervised learning looks for structure in data without being told the right answer. Here the algorithm receives the 468 clean minutes of all campuses, but not the names of the points where they were measured. The question is whether it can find the microclimates of a campus, such as shade, sun and traffic, on its own.

How many kinds of place?

Rotate the cloud. Each dot is one clean minute, placed by its temperature difference, its brightness and its PM2.5 difference. How many groups do you see?
Now color the dots by point type with the button above the scene. Shaded minutes sit at the cool and dim end, sunny minutes at the warm and bright end, and the plumes of the road and the entrance rise upwards. Some groups are clear, while others merge into each other. The number of groups is therefore a choice that the analyst has to defend, not a fact that the data hand over.

The three axes are the difference of the temperature from its campus-round median (°C), the base-10 logarithm of light (lx) and the difference of PM2.5 from its campus-round median (µg/m³). The logarithm keeps a shady 5000 lx and a sunny 60 000 lx on one readable axis. On a plain axis all shaded minutes would be squeezed into a thin strip near zero.

A few extreme minutes lie more than three standard deviations from the center. They are drawn on the edge of the box, so that the rest of the cloud stays readable. Pointing at a dot shows which visit and minute it belongs to.

2

k-means step by step

k-means places k centers in the space and gives each minute to its nearest center. It then moves every center to the mean of the minutes it received, and it repeats these two steps until no minute changes its group [1]. Before that, every channel is standardized, so that one degree of temperature and one microgram of particles count on the same scale. The buttons under the scene run these steps one by one: First 1, 2 and 3, then 2 and 3 again until the centers stop moving.

zij​=σj​xij​−μj​​d(i,c)=j=1∑3​(zij​−mcj​)2​J=i∑​d(i,c(i))2​

xij value of channel j in minute i · μj, σj mean and standard deviation of channel j · mc center of group c · c(i) the group of minute i · J total squared distance, which every step lowers.

Worked example. A sunny minute has ΔT = 3.2 °C, log₁₀ lux = 4.8 and ΔPM2.5 = 0.5. For temperature, μ = 0.15 and σ = 1.90, so z = (3.2 − 0.15) / 1.90 = 1.60. The same step for the other two channels gives the z vector (1.60, 1.02, −0.27). Its distances to the four centers of the notebook are 3.71, 1.69, 3.29 and 0.22. The smallest distance belongs to group 4, the warm and bright one, so the minute joins this group.

The chart shows two scores for every k from 2 to 6. J, the total squared distance, falls with every extra group, because more centers always lie closer to the minutes, so J alone cannot choose k. The silhouette score compares how close each minute is to its own group and to the next nearest group, on a scale from −1 to +1 [2]. Here it hardly changes between 3 and 6 groups, so several answers can be defended, and the notebook works with four groups.

3

Six channels, three axes

Principal component analysis (PCA) turns six standardized channels into new axes, ordered by how much of the variation they keep. How much of the variation can the first three axes keep?
About 85 % of the variation is kept. The channels move together: When the sun comes out, light, ultraviolet and temperature rise at the same time and humidity falls. Six channels therefore carry much less independent information than their number suggests, and three axes keep most of the story.

The bars give the share of the variation of the six standardized channels that each of the first three principal components keeps. Together they keep about 86 % of it. With the button Three principal components, the 3D scene redraws the minutes on these new axes, and its arrows show how strongly each channel loads on each component.

zi the six standardized channels of minute i · W the loadings, one column per component · si the new coordinates of the minute · λk variance along component k [4]. The first component explains 0.495 of the variation, the second 0.254 and the third 0.107.

The first component is a sunshine axis: Light, ultraviolet and the temperature difference load positively on it, and humidity negatively. This axis separates the shaded passage from the sunny square, although PCA was never told that such places exist.

4

Name the groups

k-means returns group numbers, not names. A name comes from reading the center of each group in sensor units and from the field forms of the minutes that fill it. Choose a name for each of the four groups of the notebook below, then press Check the names.

Each card shows the center of the group in sensor units and the point types that fill it.

Takeaway. A cluster is a hypothesis about the campus, not yet a finding. It becomes a finding only when the field notes, the second round or another campus show the same group again.
